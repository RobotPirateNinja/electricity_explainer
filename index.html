Volts, Amps, Ohms & Watts

{{electricity|Electricity}} is invisible, but you can see what it does. It all comes down to four ideas: how hard it’s pushed ({{voltage}}), how much flows ({{current}}), what squeezes it ({{resistance}}), and how much work it does ({{power}}).

Word detective tip: any word with a dotted underline, like {{electron}}, can be tapped or hovered to find out what it means. Words inside the bubbles work too!

1 Round and round: the circuit

Every {{wire}} is packed with {{electron|electrons}}, tiny bits of {{atom|atoms}} that can carry {{electricity}}. A {{battery}} gives them a push: they rush out of its {{negative|−}} end, all the way round the loop, and back into its {{positive|+}} end. That loop is called a {{circuit}}, and it has to be complete. One gap anywhere, and every electron stops.

Things from the kitchen drawer

Conductors and insulators

{{conductor|Conductors}} let electricity through, because their electrons are free to move. {{insulator|Insulators}} block it, because their electrons are stuck tight. Wires use both: {{copper}} inside to carry the electricity, and plastic outside to keep your fingers safe.

⚠️ Stay safe

Small batteries like AA {{cell|cells}} are safe to experiment with. The sockets in the wall are not: they push with over 100 {{volt|volts}}, enough to badly hurt or even kill. Never poke anything into a socket, and keep electricity away from water.

2 Push, flow and squeeze

Electricity is hard to picture, so here’s an {{analogy}}: imagine water flowing through a pipe. Both pictures below are controlled by the same sliders, so you can watch them side by side.

  • {{voltage|Voltage}} is the push. In the water picture, it’s how deep the tank is.
  • {{resistance|Resistance}} is the squeeze: something narrow that fights the flow.
  • {{current|Current}} is the flow: how much rushes past each second.
  • {{power|Power}} is the work being done: a glowing bulb, or a spinning water wheel.

{{voltage|Voltage}}

The push, in {{volt|volts}}. You set it with the slider.

{{resistance|Resistance}}

The squeeze, in {{ohm|ohms}}. You set this one too.

{{current|Current}}

The flow, in {{amp|amps}}. Push ÷ squeeze.

{{power|Power}}

The work, in {{watt|watts}}. Push × flow.

The big idea

More push means more flow. More squeeze means less flow. That’s {{ohms-law|Ohm’s law}}, and it works for every circuit there is. Try it: keep the push the same and slide the squeeze up and down.

Why do things get hot?

When electrons are forced through {{resistance}}, they bump and jostle and some of their {{energy}} turns into {{heat}}. That’s how a bulb’s {{filament}} glows, and how a toaster toasts. With almost no resistance at all you get a {{short-circuit}}, and that’s dangerous.

3 Watts around the house

Every {{appliance}} has a {{power}} rating in {{watt|watts}}: how much electricity it uses every second it’s switched on. Things that make {{heat}} use the most. Pick one and compare it with a little {{led|LED}} light bulb.

4 How they’re connected

Voltage, current, resistance and power are a team. If you know two of them, you can always work out the others. Cover one letter with your finger and the triangle tells you the {{formula}}. The numbers come straight from the lab in step 2.
